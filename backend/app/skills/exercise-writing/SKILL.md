# Writing grammar questions

A good question can only be answered by someone who knows the grammar point, and has
exactly one right answer (or, for open questions, a clear set of right answers). Most
bad questions fail in one of three ways: the task gives the answer away, a second
answer also fits, or the sentence is unnatural.

## Work in this order

1. Pick a short, everyday situation and write the **full correct sentence** first.
2. Decide what the learner must produce or spot: the part that needs the grammar point.
3. Write the task around it, then **check before you fix the key**: read the task as a
   learner who does not know the rule. What else could they write, choose or mark, and
   still be right? If something else fits, change the context or the cue until it
   does not; only then write the answer.
4. Check that the rest of the sentence uses words at or below the learner's level, and
   that nothing in it is a trap on a different grammar point.

## Never give the tested form away

The instruction, the hint and the required opening must not contain the form the
learner is being tested on, or tell them where it goes. If they could copy the answer
from the task, the question tests nothing.

- Bad: "Add *always* after *are*." (the position is the rule being tested)
- Bad: "Rewrite using *bored*." (choosing bored / boring is the rule being tested)
- Bad: "Start with *The girl who lives next door*." (the relative clause is the answer)
- Good: give the meaning or the situation, and let the learner choose the form:
  "Say the same thing using *always*." / "How does the boy feel? Use an adjective
  made from *bore*." / "Join the two sentences into one."

A hint may give the base word to put in the right form (*close*, *know*) - that is
what the learner has to change. It must not be the changed form itself.

## Match the kind of error to the format

- An error of **form** (wrong ending, wrong tense, wrong word order, wrong pronoun) suits
  every format.
- A **missing** word (a dropped auxiliary, a missing article) has no single wrong
  piece to point at: do not use `find_fix` for it. Use `cloze` (the gap is where the
  word goes) or `transform`.
- A contrast between two forms (bored / boring, past simple / present perfect) suits
  `choice4` or `cloze`, with a context that makes only one of them right.

## One right answer

- Read the whole sentence, not just the gap: a time phrase, a second clause or a reply
  often decides the form. Add one when the sentence alone allows two forms.
- Lock what the learner cannot know from the grammar: positive or negative, the
  number of words, which verb to use (*will* or *be going to* when both are fine).
- For open questions, list every natural right answer you can think of, best first,
  and make sure each of them uses the grammar point (not a different structure that
  also means the same).

## Natural English

Short sentences people really say or write, in a situation a learner recognises. No
trick questions, no rare words, nothing culturally specific, nothing private.

The details for each format are in this skill's references: `choice4`, `cloze`,
`find-fix`, `transform`, `translate`, `rewrite-own`, and for questions that check
understanding of a rule, `understanding-checks`, `concept-choice` and `explain`.
